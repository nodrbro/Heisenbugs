What makes an alert get escalated?

Exploratory analysis and modelling notes for the financial-monitoring alert challenge (team BB5FBD89). We estimate the probability that a specialist escalates each alert, using only that customer's 180-day transaction history. The data are synthetic.

Approach

The data are relational: each alert has hundreds of transactions behind it. We turn each history into one row of behavioural features and train a gradient-boosted tree model on those rows. It is scored by ROC-AUC, so only the ranking of the predicted probabilities matters.

  1. Profile the data. Check the schema, the time coverage relative to the alert date, whether train and test come from the same distribution, and the target rate over time.
  2. Compare escalated and dismissed customers on counts, direction × type mix, amount distributions, timing, and the activity just before the alert.
  3. Engineer 329 features per alert in 11 families. The families come from the EDA findings below.
  4. Train LightGBM with strong regularization (7 leaves, 200-sample leaves, 20% feature sampling). Validate with stratified 5-fold CV repeated over 5 seeds, and rank-average the test predictions.

Dataset overview

Two signal tables (14,000 train / 6,000 test alerts) and two transaction tables (6,987,663 / 3,027,575 rows). Every alert has at least one transaction. Each history covers exactly the 180 days before the alert date. Transactions are 76% incoming. Type mix: karta 53.8%, bank_otkazmasi 39.4%, naqd 6.3%, xalqaro 0.5%.

Target distribution

17.2% of training alerts are escalated, a moderate class imbalance. That is not a problem for ROC-AUC, so we don't resample. Train and test alert dates span the same two years with the same distribution. The escalation rate is flat across quarters and weekdays, so the split is random rather than by time, and the alert date itself carries almost no signal. Adversarial validation (a model trained to tell train from test) scores AUC ≈ 0.50, which confirms there is no distribution shift, so plain stratified K-fold is a valid proxy for the hidden test set.

Transaction activity over time

Activity is not uniform over the 180-day window. It peaks 3–5 months before the alert at about 3.2 transactions per day, then tapers steadily to about 1.0 per day in the final days. Escalated customers are slightly more active at every point. Then comes a burst right before the alert: 99% of alerts have a block of transactions stamped in the final minutes before midnight on the day before the alert (median 31 transactions, 7.9% of all rows). The burst is a little more card-heavy than the rest of the history (60% vs 53% card), but it has almost the same size and mix for both classes, so the burst features we built add little signal.

Incoming vs outgoing, and transaction types

The key finding came from moving from counts to money. When we weight each transaction by an estimate of its real amount, escalated customers push much more of their value through outgoing card payments and cash deposits, and less through bank transfers.

Transaction-size distributions

miqdor_indeksi behaves like a standardized log-amount. Two size effects: escalated customers' bank transfers are smaller, and they more often make micro card payments at the very bottom of the index. Micro payments often come in groups and near midnight, similar to card-testing behaviour.

From EDA to features

  • Money, not countsDirection × type value shares, weighted by exp(2·index). This scale was chosen by scanning k (chart above). They are the strongest family.
  • Per-cell amount statisticsMean, median, standard deviation, min, max and quantile-bin shares for each of the 8 direction × type cells. These capture the smaller bank transfers and the card-amount floor.
  • Micro card paymentsCount and share of card payments with index < −2.2, split by direction.
  • Recency windowsCounts, sums and mean amounts over 1–90 days before the alert, plus recent-vs-baseline rate ratios and a comparison of the first and second halves of the history.
  • Pre-alert burstSize, amount shift and type mix of the pre-midnight block compared with the customer's baseline.
  • Timing & sequenceTime between transactions and how bursty it is, busiest single day, count and amount trends, pass-through (money going out soon after it comes in), direction switches, night and weekend share.

Model & validation

LightGBM with a binary objective, learning rate 0.005, 7 leaves, at least 200 samples per leaf, 20% of features sampled per tree, L2 = 20, and early stopping on each fold's AUC. The signal is weak and spread across many features, so heavy regularization beat larger trees. Blending in HistGradientBoosting or logistic regression did not improve out-of-fold AUC, so the final model is LightGBM alone, rank-averaged over 5 seeds × 5 folds. Out-of-fold ROC-AUC: 0.6516 (per fold 0.6499 ± 0.0104).

Conclusion

  • Signal lives in the history, not the date.The escalation rate is flat over time, and train and test are indistinguishable (adversarial AUC ≈ 0.50).
  • Where the money goes matters most.Value share of outgoing card payments and cash deposits pushes escalation up; bank-transfer share pushes it down.
  • Size tells a story.Smaller bank transfers and micro card payments at the index floor are both linked to escalation.
  • Timing is structured but not predictive.Activity peaks months before the alert and tapers off, and almost every history ends with a dense pre-midnight burst. Both patterns look the same for escalated and dismissed alerts.
  • Weak signal, spread across many features.No single feature exceeds AUC 0.60. A heavily regularized tree ensemble combines them to about 0.65 ROC-AUC, stable across folds.
